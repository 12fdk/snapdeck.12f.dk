You sit down to study, pick the biggest task, and tell yourself you are going to work for two hours. Forty minutes in you are reading a sentence three times.

The whole answer, in one go. Work in blocks of ten to twenty minutes of actual work, and end each block before your focus does. Put a timer where you can see it, because a visible end point changes how a task feels. Take a real break between blocks: five to ten minutes away from the material.

Make the first step of a session tiny, so small that starting is never the hard part, and decide tomorrow's first step the night before. Aim to make each block a retrieval block, one where you answer things yourself. The design does the holding: the session runs on structure and a timer, and your willpower stays in the bank.

## What actually happens when you try to force a long session

Three things work against a long block, and none of them are laziness. If evenings like this are every week and getting worse, a doctor is the person to talk to, and this post is for the setup, not the diagnosis.

**Open loops.** A task with no visible end point is the one an ADHD brain struggles to hold. 'Finish revision for chapter 4' is a whole exam sitting in your head, and the open loop eats your working memory all evening. A timer gives the loop a hard end, and with the end visible, the task becomes 'do this until the beep'.

**The start is the peak.** The hardest moment of studying tends to be the first five minutes, and for you the start is the problem, because once you are inside the material, the material usually holds you. Most of the design below is about making the start frictionless, because that is where the evening is decided.

**The task is a wall, not a step.** 'Revise for the biology exam' is a subject, not a task. A wall-shaped task produces a freeze: the brain has to plan the whole thing before it can touch any of it. Split it into steps small enough to do without a plan, and the freeze tends to clear.

## How long should a study session be if you cannot focus?

The length is a measurement, not a decision, and you measure it by what happens at the end of the block. Set a block at ten to twenty minutes. If you finish it with steam to spare, lengthen it by five next time. If you keep dying in the middle, it is too long, and shortening it is the only correct fix.

The break is half the technique. A real break means you leave the material: stand up, water, a minute at the window. Five to ten minutes is enough. Scrolling your phone isn't a break in the useful sense, because the next stimulus is always one tap away, and the session dies the moment you pick the phone up.

## How does the Pomodoro technique work when your attention is shorter than 25 minutes?

Pomodoro is 25 minutes on, 5 off, four rounds, long break. Run it at your own numbers: a 15 by 5 block is a Pomodoro, and a 10 by 3 block is a Pomodoro. The name doesn't care about the length.

Two rules keep it working:

1. **The break is a break.** Five minutes away from the material. No 'just one more card'. The loop has to close for the next block to start clean.
2. **A broken block is still a win.** If the timer hits and you are halfway through a paragraph, stop. The block worked. Only negotiate a second round when you genuinely want it, because negotiating is how 15 minutes becomes 'I sat for an hour and did nothing'.

### One evening, rebuilt

Here is a concrete version, because the setup sounds thin until you run it. Say you have a 6 pm to 9 pm window and a biology exam next week:

1. Before you sit down, write the one topic you are doing tonight: 'Mitochondria and the electron transport chain'. One line on a card. The other topics wait until tomorrow.
2. Set the timer for 15 minutes. Put the phone in another room, or at least face-down across the desk.
3. When the timer goes off, stand up for 5 minutes. Water, window, done.
4. Repeat. Four to five blocks is a real evening of study. If you get three, that is still three, and it is more than the zero the old setup was producing.
5. Before you stop for the night, write one sentence: what the next block opens with. 'Write the card for the electron carriers' is a sentence tomorrow's you can act on without thinking.

Step five is the quiet one that makes the whole thing stick. It turns 'I have to figure out what to do when I sit down' into 'I have a sentence to read', and the second version is the one that actually happens.

## What to remove from the first five minutes

This section decides the evening, because every item on the list sits between 'I decided to study' and 'I am doing it'. Remove each one this week:

1. **The decision of what to do.** Decide it the day before. Tomorrow's first block has a one-line task written before today is over, and the morning version of you isn't the version that should be choosing.
2. **The hunt for the materials.** The notebook, the PDF, the specific page, the pen that works. Lay them out the night before, in the order the block uses them. A five-minute hunt is a start you will not have.
3. **The wall-shaped task.** Before: 'revise chapter 4'. After, written at the top of the page: 'read pages 41 to 44, underline the bold terms, write a card for each'. The first step has to be doable without a plan. For a chemistry lab report the same split looks like: 'transcribe the three results tables, then draft the one-paragraph summary'. The subject changes, the shape of the split doesn't.
4. **The phone in reach.** Out of the room is the version that works. Face-down across the desk is the version that works sometimes. In your hand is the version that ends the session.
5. **The need to be in the right mood.** You don't have to feel like studying to start. The feeling usually arrives somewhere inside the first block, and waiting for it before you sit down is waiting for a condition that does not arrive.

None of these costs anything. All of them are setup you do once, and the evening stops deciding whether it is going to happen.

## Where short blocks do not go far enough

The setup above gets you through the evening. Two changes extend it to the semester.

**Make the material active.** A block of re-reading is a block of passive time, and passive time is where studying stops landing. The testing effect is the finding here: Roediger and Karpicke showed in 2006 that taking a test on the material beats spending the same time re-studying it.

Flashcards are the cheapest active format that fits a short block, because each card is one quick retrieval. Try it: for a pharmacology chapter, one card per drug, the name on one side, the mechanism and the one side effect that gets tested on the other. That is a deck of ten to twenty cards for a fifteen-minute block.

The format works handwritten, it works in a free app, and it works with a photo-to-cards tool. Photograph a page of notes or a whiteboard and get a deck back in seconds, which removes the 'I have to make the cards' friction from the start of a block. SnapDeck AI does that on an iPhone, and a pen with a stack of index cards does it for free. Both beat re-reading. Why retrieval beats re-reading is in the [active recall post](/blog/active-recall-explained/).

**Come back to the material on a different day.** The spacing effect, reviewed by Cepeda and colleagues in 2006, says the same material studied across separate days lands better than one long sitting. Concretely: revisit last night's cards for one block tomorrow. Ten minutes of yesterday's deck is the single cheapest session in your week, and it is the one that tends to save the rest. Short blocks make this easy, because a block is small enough to fit in a gap.

The [focus post](/blog/focus-when-you-cant/) has the surrounding setup, and the [habit post](/blog/build-the-study-habit/) has the cue side of the problem, which is where a daily block of 15 minutes at the same time of day actually sticks.

## What to do when a session is going badly

A session goes badly in two ways, and they need different fixes.

**You cannot start.** Don't negotiate with the whole session. Start the five-minute version: open the notebook, read one paragraph, write one card. It often turns into more, and if it doesn't, it still counts, because the alternative is zero, and a five-minute session keeps the habit alive for the next day. The [habit post](/blog/build-the-study-habit/) covers the 'ten minutes or stop' rule in more detail, and the [focus post](/blog/focus-when-you-cant/) covers the environment side of a start that will not happen.

**You are inside the material but not learning it.** This is the passive-reading version. Close the book. Write down everything you remember from the last block, from nothing, in one minute. Then open it and check. The gap between what you wrote and what is in the book is the session's actual content, and it is also the content the next block should open with. That is the retrieval pass in its smallest form, and it works on any material, flashcard-shaped or not.

## One more thing before you start

The one principle that outranks the rest: a short session that is active beats a long session that is passive, for the way a single block uses your time. The length isn't the point. Retrieval is. If the evening only allows one block, make that block a retrieval block, and skip the re-reading.
