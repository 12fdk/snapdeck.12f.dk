The night-before-the-exam survival guide

You have one evening and more material than time. This is the triage plan — no lectures about how you should have started in September.

It's the evening before. You are not going to learn the module tonight. Accept that in the first five minutes and you free up the energy to do the thing that actually helps: maximise the marks available from what you can still fit in your head.

That's a different job from studying, and it has a different method.

First: the one rule

Tonight, every minute you spend reading is a minute you didn't spend retrieving. Re-reading is comfortable, feels productive, and is close to worthless at this range — you'll recognise everything and be able to produce none of it.

The entire plan below is built on one swap: turn material into questions, then answer the questions. Nothing else earns its place tonight.

If you only read one line Cover the answer. Say it out loud. Check. Repeat on the ones you got wrong. That is the whole method.

Hour 1 — Triage, not studying

Don't open a textbook yet. Spend the first hour deciding what you're studying, because the wrong choice here costs more than anything else tonight.

  • Find out what's on the exam. Past papers, the module outline, the "exam format" slide everyone ignored. Ten minutes here can eliminate a third of the material.
  • Split the topics into three piles. Know it, half-know it, never seen it.
  • Work the middle pile first. This is the big one. Half-known topics convert to marks fastest — you're closing a gap, not starting from zero. The "never seen it" pile is the most expensive per mark, and the "know it" pile is a comfort blanket.
  • Weight by marks. A 30-mark topic you half-know beats three 5-mark topics you know nothing about.

Write the resulting list down. Three to five topics, in order. That list is your evening.

Hours 2–4 — Convert, then quiz

For each topic on the list, in order:

  1. Turn it into questions. Twenty to thirty per topic, straight from your notes, the slides, the textbook headings. Definitions, mechanisms, dates, formulas, "compare X and Y".
  2. Answer them without looking. Out loud or on paper — writing beats thinking-you-know-it.
  3. Sort as you go. Got it / didn't. Only the "didn't" pile comes round again.
  4. Second pass on the misses. Then a third. The pile shrinks fast, and watching it shrink is worth more for morale than any amount of highlighting.

Step one is where most people's evening dies. Writing thirty questions by hand takes forty minutes of an evening you don't have, so it gets skipped and the night collapses back into re-reading. This is exactly what SnapDeck AI is for: photograph the pages, get the question-and-answer cards, start quizzing within a minute or two. It runs on the phone with no connection, which also means it works when you've relocated to a corridor with no wifi.

Use timers. Twenty-five minutes on, five off. Not because it's trendy — because at hour three your judgement about "just one more page" gets unreliable.

Hour 5 — The high-yield sweep

Last working hour. Do not start a new topic. Instead:

  • Run the misses pile one final time.
  • Write out anything you'll want in the first two minutes of the exam — formulas, a framework, the six steps of a process. Not to smuggle in, but because writing it once more from memory tonight makes it far more likely to be there tomorrow.
  • Skim the "never seen it" pile for headline definitions only. Knowing what a term means is often worth a mark or two; understanding it fully is not happening tonight.

Then go to sleep. Genuinely.

This is the part everyone wants to negotiate with, so here's the plain version: sleep is when the brain consolidates what you studied. Skipping it doesn't just make you tired, it removes the step that files the evening's work away — and it degrades exactly the things an exam measures: recall, reasoning, and staying calm.

Six or seven hours of sleep will beat two extra hours of studying followed by four hours of sleep. It isn't close. If you're choosing between finishing the misses pile and going to bed, go to bed.

An all-nighter converts material you sort-of learned into material you sort-of learned and can't access. It's the worst trade available on the menu.

The morning of

  • Eat something. Boring, true.
  • Do one quick pass of your cards — twenty minutes, misses only. Not new material. This is a warm-up, not a rescue.
  • Stop 30 minutes before. Cramming in the corridor mostly manufactures panic, and panic costs more marks than the last three facts would have earned.
  • In the first minute of the exam, write down anything you're scared of forgetting. Get it out of working memory and onto the paper.

What to skip tonight, guilt-free

  • Rewriting your notes. It's transcription with extra steps.
  • Colour-coding anything. Zero marks available.
  • Reading the textbook chapter. Too slow, too passive, wrong tool.
  • Topics you've never seen that are worth few marks. Let them go. Deciding not to study something is a legitimate move.
  • Comparing plans with whoever is posting about their revision timetable. They're also panicking.

And afterwards

Tonight is damage control, and damage control has a ceiling. The reason this evening is hard isn't that you're lazy — it's that the material only got turned into questions at the last possible moment.

The fix isn't a personality transplant. It's doing the conversion step earlier: photograph each week's notes as you go, so by the time the next exam looms you already have the decks and the night before is just a review session. Same effort, spread out, dramatically better outcome. There's a whole post on why that works — read it next week, though. Tonight you have a misses pile to shrink.

The short version: triage for an hour, convert to questions, answer them, sleep properly, warm up in the morning, stop early. Good luck — you'll be fine.
