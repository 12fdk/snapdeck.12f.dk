How to catch up on a semester of notes you never reopened

You have three months of lecture notes, PDFs, and photographed whiteboards piling up in front of you. The first step is not to study — it is to sort, triage, and pick the twenty percent that will actually get asked.

By Robert JensenFounder, 12F ApS

Short answer: Start by sorting, not studying. Rank every topic by how heavily it is examined and how confident you are, cover the high-weight gaps first, and turn each one into questions you can test yourself on. Accept that some low-weight material will stay shallow — a deliberate gap beats an untouched pile.

There is a specific kind of dread that comes from opening a drawer full of lecture notes and realising you have not looked at a single one since the first week of term. Three months of PDFs, photographed whiteboards, and handwritten pages sit on your desk and multiply every time you walk past. The bigger problem is not the material — it is the paralysis of not knowing where to begin.

You are not behind because you are lazy. You are behind because the standard advice — read the notes, highlight the key points, make a plan — falls apart the moment you face a semester's worth of content that feels like a different subject each week. What you need is not motivation. You need triage.

Stop treating revision like a race

The most common mistake students make when they finally sit down to catch up is to start at the beginning. They open the first lecture, read from page one, and pretend to make notes. It feels productive because they are technically studying. It is also one of the slowest ways to prepare for an exam.

Every hour spent studying early lecture material is an hour not spent on material that is more likely to be tested. The end of a course usually contains the synthesis — the topics that tie everything together, the ones the lecturer spent the most time on, and the ones most likely to carry the heaviest weight in an exam. Starting at the end is not a hack. It is basic resource allocation.

Think of it like this: if you have four weeks until an exam and three months of material, you cannot cover everything. The question is not whether you will cover everything — you will not — the question is whether the material you do cover is the material the exam actually tests. The answer to that depends entirely on where you start.

How do you triage a semester of notes?

Before you study a single page, spend twenty minutes sorting your material into three piles:

Essential. Topics the lecturer emphasised, topics that appeared in tutorials or labs, past paper questions that map to these lectures, and topics that connect to three or more other lectures. These are the ones worth the most marks.

Useful. Topics that were covered but are either minor standalone items or prerequisites for essential topics. You will study these after the essentials are solid.

Skip. Decorative lectures, optional reading that was never tested before, and material that overlaps significantly with something in the essential pile. Mark them, set them aside, and do not look at them until you have time to spare.

You can do this triage by skimming each lecture's title, learning outcomes, and final slide — usually the one that says "summary" or "this week in review." Ten minutes per lecture. Thirty lectures. Five hours total. That is the cost of triage. The alternative cost is weeks of studying material the exam does not test.

Should you summarise your notes or turn them into questions?

Once you have identified your essential lectures, the next step is to convert them into something you can actively test on. This is where most students stall — they stare at pages of dense text and think they need to rewrite it, summarise it, or understand it fully before they start studying. None of that is true.

The fastest path from material to recall is a two-step process: photograph the page, then turn the content into questions.

A photograph of a dense textbook page or a lecture handout is enough. A photo-to-flashcard tool — like SnapDeck AI, which generates question-and-answer cards from a picture in seconds — does the heavy lifting of turning statements into recall questions. You get a deck of cards. You start flipping through them. If you get a card wrong, that is the signal to revisit that specific point. If you get it right, move on.

The process mirrors the system described in how to memorise a lot of material fast — turn material into questions, test yourself, identify the gaps. The only difference here is that you are working with a much larger body of material, so speed matters. You cannot spend an hour per lecture writing cards by hand. You need something faster, and a photo-to-flashcard tool gives you that speed without sacrificing the quality of the questions.

Of course, a photo-to-flashcard tool is not magic. It extracts questions from text, but it does not know which questions are examinable and which are filler. Your brain still needs to make that call. The tool is a speed multiplier for a method that already works on paper — it just means you get to the testing phase in minutes rather than hours.

Build a realistic schedule, not an ideal one

This is the part students resist the most: you cannot cover three months of material in two weeks. And you should not try.

A realistic catch-up plan has two phases:

Phase one — essentials only. Your goal is to build a solid working knowledge of the topics most likely to be tested. This means triage, questions, flashcards, and active recall — not reading everything. You should be able to look at a past paper question on an essential topic and answer it without hesitation. That is the target.

Phase two — useful material. Once the essentials are solid, fill in gaps from the useful pile. You are not trying to master these topics — you are trying to make sure you are not caught flat-footed if an unexpected question appears.

Phase three — only if you have time. Skip pile. If you have a buffer week or an exam that feels secure, you can return to the skipped material. Most students never reach this phase, and that is fine.

Here is the thing about realistic scheduling: it feels like admitting defeat at first. You did not sign up for this course to do a partial cover. But an exam is not a completion certificate — it is a test of what you can do with the material under pressure. You pass by demonstrating knowledge of the examinable core, not by pretending to have read everything.

The psychology of starting late

The reason a semester of notes feels insurmountable is not the volume. It is the guilt. You know you left them. You know you could have started earlier. And every page on the desk is a reminder of the time you wasted.

This guilt is productive if it drives you to start. It is destructive if it keeps you paralysed. Most students sit in the destructive phase longer than they should — telling themselves they will start tomorrow, that they need to organise their desk first, that they need to find the right notes before they can begin. None of that is real. The right notes are the ones in front of you. The right time is now.

The first lecture is always the hardest. You open it, feel the weight of everything behind you, and want to close it again. Do it anyway. Turn it into five questions. Answer those five questions. Close it. Tomorrow, do another one. The guilt shrinks with every lecture you actually work through. By the end of the week, the pile of unopened lectures will be smaller, and the feeling of panic will have been replaced by something you can actually work with: a set of flashcards, a growing sense of what the exam will look like, and the confidence that comes from having started.

When should you push through, and when should you stop?

There is a difference between being behind and being in a hole you cannot climb out of. Most students are behind — they just did not start soon enough, and a focused triage approach will get them through. A smaller number are in a genuine hole: multiple failed components, severe time pressure (less than a week), or material that genuinely cannot be covered in the time available.

If you are in the second category, the approach changes. You stop trying to cover anything and go purely strategic. Past papers become your syllabus. Every question on every past paper maps to a specific topic. You identify the topics that appear most frequently and only study those. Everything else is a bonus. This is not ideal, but it is honest about the constraints you are working under.

The one thing that actually works

Every method described above — triage, question conversion, active recall, realistic scheduling — is just structure. The thing that makes any of it work is a single habit: start now. Not tomorrow. Not after you have organised your notes. Now.

Open one lecture. Spend fifteen minutes on it. Turn it into questions. Answer those questions. Close it. You have just done what three months of procrastination could not do: you have begun.

The student who covers five lectures this week and builds real recall from them will outperform the student who pretends to cover twenty. The student who starts today will not be looking at a mountain of notes three weeks from now.

The notes you never reopened do not define how well you do on the exam. They define what you started with. Everything after that is just a series of choices — where to begin, what to focus on, when to stop pretending and start studying. The first choice is the simplest: open the drawer, pick one lecture, and begin.

Common questions

Where do I even start when there is so much material?

Not at the beginning. Starting at the first lecture is a trap — it feels productive but wastes time on material you will never be tested on. Start at the end instead: look at the learning outcomes, past papers, or the final week's lecture, which usually contains the topics worth the most marks. Work backwards from what the exam actually tests rather than forwards through everything you have ever been given.

Is it better to read everything first or jump straight into questions?

Neither extreme works on its own. You need a quick scan — ten to fifteen minutes per lecture, just enough to tag each one as essential, useful, or skip — and then move to questions on the essential material first. Reading everything before studying is one of the most common ways students waste time. You do not need to understand every page before you start testing yourself.

How many lectures should I try to cover each day?

Quality matters far more than quantity. Covering two lectures thoroughly — turning them into questions and actually testing yourself — will always beat skimming ten lectures and remembering nothing. When you are behind, the temptation is to treat revision like a race. It is not. Every hour spent on material you can recall is an hour the exam cannot take from you. Every hour spent pretending to cover material is lost.

What if I cannot tell which topics are important?

Look for signals: topics repeated across multiple lectures, topics the lecturer emphasised verbally, topics that connect to others in the course, and anything that appeared in a tutorial or lab. If you have past papers, the pattern is usually clear — three or four big topics appear almost every year. When in doubt, prioritise the later lectures; exam boards tend to weight the end of the course more heavily.

Do I need to rewrite my notes before studying?

No. Rewriting is a classic productivity illusion — it feels like progress, but you are just transcribing someone else's understanding. Turn the material into questions and test yourself on the original notes instead. If your handwriting is illegible or the notes are messy, photograph the page and use a photo-to-flashcard tool to generate clean cards. The goal is active recall, not pretty notes.

How do I stop feeling overwhelmed once I start?

It usually fades once you begin. The fear of the mountain is always bigger than the mountain itself. Pick one lecture, turn it into five questions, answer those five questions, and stop for the day. Tomorrow you do another one. Momentum builds from action, not from planning. By the third or fourth day you will have a working deck of cards and a rhythm that feels manageable — and the overwhelm that made you freeze in the first place will have disappeared.
