SnapDeck AI vs Quizlet, Anki & Brainscape

You do not need the most powerful flashcard app. You need one you will open the night before the exam. Here is what the four options you will actually find are built to do.

By Robert JensenFounder, 12F ApS

Short answer: Pick the app that matches how you study. Quizlet and Cram are the easiest to start and the best for a quick exam. Anki is the most powerful and the steadiest for long-term recall. Brainscape sits in the middle. SnapDeck AI is the fastest way to get cards from a photo or a PDF.

You are not choosing the smartest app. You are choosing the one you will open at 9 p.m. the night before the exam, and the one that turns a pile of notes into cards without eating your evening. That is the only decision that matters, and it is why "which one is best" is the wrong question.

This list is the one students actually search for: Quizlet, Anki, Brainscape and Cram, plus a fifth path — making the cards from a photo instead of by hand. I will describe what each is genuinely built to do, what it costs, and the one requirement that quietly rules some people out. No app is best for everyone, and the honest answer is a fork in the road.

What are you actually buying when you pick a flashcard app?

Two things. The scheduling — the system that decides which card to show you next, and when. And the setup cost — how many minutes stand between you and a usable deck.

Most of the learning that makes flashcards worth it comes from self-testing and from spacing, the idea reviewed by Cepeda and colleagues in 2006: the same material is remembered better when the reviews are spread out than when they are crammed into one sitting. Retrieval practice is the engine, and the research behind it is real — Roediger and Karpicke showed in 2006 that testing yourself beats re-reading. So a flashcard app is only as good as the scheduling behind it and as easy as the path from "here is my material" to "here is a card I can be tested on."

That is the whole comparison in one line: every app on this list trades scheduling strength against setup speed.

Quizlet and Cram: the easy start

These two are the most approachable, and the difference between them is small. Quizlet is the biggest name; it has many study modes — flip, match, a timed game, a practice test — and a very large library of decks other people have already made. Cram is the smaller, simpler sibling: mostly flip cards and a shuffle, with less going on.

Both are free to start, and both gate the useful extras — offline access, an ad-free experience, more powerful studying — behind a paid tier. Both are built around typing a list or importing one, or finding a pre-made deck. The catch with pre-made decks is that they are not checked; a deck someone else wrote can be wrong, and you will study their mistake as if it were yours.

Pick one of these when the exam is close, the material is already in your head as a list, and you want to start in two minutes. If you find yourself typing a whole semester of notes into boxes by hand, that is the setup cost you will feel the most.

Anki: the most powerful, the steepest climb

Anki is the one serious students and medics reach for, and for a reason. Its scheduling is the most robust of the group — it is the open-source implementation that a lot of the research on spaced repetition is run against — and it is the most tunable. You can set the intervals, format the cards, and extend it with a large community of add-ons. It is free on desktop and on Android; the iPhone app is a one-time purchase rather than a subscription. If you are deciding whether the power is worth the setup at all, the write-up of active recall is the clearest place to see why the scheduling matters more than the app that serves it.

The cost is setup. Anki rewards patience and punishes people who want to start tonight. The interface is functional, not pretty, and the power comes from decisions you make about card types, tags and defaults before a single card is in the deck. If you have the time and you are studying a long, content-heavy course, that investment usually pays off. If your exam is in a week, it probably does not.

Brainscape: the middle ground

Brainscape sits between the two poles. Its scheduling uses a confidence rating — you mark how sure you were on each answer from one to five — which is a nice fit for the kind of conceptual material where "sort of got it" is the honest answer. It is easier than Anki and offers curated, expert-checked classes in some subjects. It is a subscription, and the useful limits on the free tier are real.

How to choose: the fork in the road

  • Exam in days, material already list-shaped. Quizlet or Cram. Start in two minutes, forget the rest.
  • A long, content-heavy course you will keep for a career. Anki. Pay the setup cost once.
  • Conceptual material where confidence, not recall, is the signal. Brainscape.
  • The setup is the wall. If the reason you have not started is that making the cards by hand takes longer than studying, the path you are missing is a photo-to-cards app. Photograph the page, the notes or the whiteboard, import a PDF, and the question-and-answer cards come back in seconds. That is the whole gap it closes, and it is why it is the only entry on this list that is not a typing app.

One honest sentence, and then I will leave it with you: SnapDeck AI is the photo-to-cards path — an iPhone app that turns a photo or a PDF into question-and-answer cards, with the AI running on the device so nothing is uploaded. It is free for a few decks a month and then a one-time purchase, not a subscription. The catch, said plainly, is that it needs a recent iPhone with the on-device AI enabled, so it genuinely rules some readers out. If that is you, Quizlet or Anki are the honest picks, and a pen and a stack of paper will still work.

How long before an exam should you start?

Start the day the cards exist, not the day the topic is due. The spacing that makes recall stick is wasted if the deck only appears the week before. A deck you build on Monday and review for two weeks beats a deck you build the night before. That is the spacing effect doing its work, and it is the same reason a small, early review beat a big, late one in the research above.

FAQ

Does the free tier matter more than the price? Sometimes. An app that is free but hides its scheduling behind a paywall is a different tool than one that is paid up front and does not change. Compare what you actually get on the free tier, not the sticker price.

Can I use more than one of these? Yes, and a lot of students do: a fast app for a coming exam and a powerful one for the long course. The only thing to avoid is four apps and no reviews.

Will pre-made decks save me time? They can, for a first pass. But they are written by strangers and not checked, so treat them as a draft to fix, not an authority to trust.

Is a subscription worth it? Only if you will use the features it unlocks every week. If you will study in short, occasional bursts, a one-time purchase — or a free tier that does what you need — usually beats paying monthly for the rest of the semester you will not open it in.

Common questions

Is Anki really better than Quizlet at long-term memory?

For high-volume, long-term recall, Anki's scheduling is the most robust and the most tunable of the four. Quizlet is easier to start and fine for a coming exam, but its scheduling is less developed. The best one is the one you will actually open.

Can I make flashcards without typing every card by hand?

That is the gap between the options. Quizlet and Cram are mostly built around typing or importing a list. Anki can be set up to take images but the setup is a project. A photo-to-cards app closes that gap directly.

Which flashcard app is free?

Anki is free on desktop and on Android; its iPhone app is a one-time purchase. Quizlet, Cram and Brainscape are free to start but lock useful features behind a subscription. One app, SnapDeck AI, is free for a few decks a month and then a one-time buy.

Do I need an internet connection or an account?

Most of the big apps want a connection and, for sync, an account. The photo-to-cards app in this list runs entirely on the device: no account, nothing uploaded, works offline. The trade-off is it only runs on a recent iPhone with the on-device AI enabled.
